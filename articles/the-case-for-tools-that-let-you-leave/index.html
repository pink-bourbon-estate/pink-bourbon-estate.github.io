The case for tools that let you leave

Most software wants you to stay. The small tools made here try to do the opposite: to be useful, to be understood, and to be easy to walk away from.

The quiet cost of free

A great deal of everyday software is free to use and paid for in other ways. It watches what you click, remembers who you are, builds a profile, and uses that to sell your attention to someone else. Other software is the reverse: honest about the price, but a subscription for something that used to be a one-off purchase, with your own files locked behind it.

Neither is a conspiracy. They are what happens when a tool is a business first. But a person who just wants to plan a bake, find a song, learn a chess opening or practise a scale does not need to be tracked to do it, or to pay every month to keep doing it. Most of these jobs are small. They deserve small tools.

What a small, honest tool looks like

  • It runs on your machine. Once you have it, it works offline, today and in ten years, whether or not the website is still there.
  • It collects nothing. Not "anonymised", not "to improve the service": nothing. There are no accounts to make, so there is nothing to leak.
  • It costs nothing and is not for sale. No paywall, no premium tier, no advert.
  • You can read it. Every line of code carries a plain-language comment saying what it is for and why, so a curious beginner can follow along, and it can be changed to fit how you actually work.
  • You can take it and go. It is free to fork, change and share, with credit. If this project vanished tomorrow, your copy would carry on.

An honest note about AI

I cannot write code. The tools here are built with the help of AI, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. What matters is how that help is used.

There are two ways to use an AI system. One is as an oracle you consult for every answer, forever: a dependency that grows. The other is as a workshop: you describe what you need, the machine helps build it, a person checks that it does what it claims, and then the finished thing stands on its own. It is commented so it can be understood, and it runs without the machine that helped to make it.

This estate is built on the second approach. The point is not to repeat the same AI work over and over, but to do it once, carefully, and keep the result. That reduces reliance rather than increasing it, and it frees time and attention for the next idea, or for nothing at all. A person in the loop is not decoration. They decide what is worth building, ask what the code is doing until it makes sense, and refuse anything that tracks, hides or locks in.

If a tool made this way helps someone realise they can do a job without reaching for an AI, a subscription or an account, then it has done what it was for.

Privacy as a design choice, not a policy

The strongest privacy promise is not a long document. It is a design in which the thing could not happen. A website with no scripts from other places cannot send your visit to them. A program that never opens a network connection cannot send your data anywhere. A tool with no accounts has nothing to breach. Where possible, the promise is built into how the thing works, and then written down so it can be checked.

That is why the sites here load no fonts, libraries or analytics from other places, set no cookies, and keep all searching and filtering inside your own browser. When there is a link to another service, it is an ordinary link that you choose to click. It is not an embedded player that tells someone you were here.

Gathering information the polite way

Some tools need facts from the outside world. The rule is to take only what is public and factual, ask slowly, ask once, read the site's rules first, say who is asking, give credit, and take things down if asked. The article on building the Radio 6 Music Collection shows what that looks like in practice.

Taking tools offline

Everything here is meant to work without the internet once you have it. That is not nostalgia. It means the tool is yours: it does not stop when a company changes its plans, and it does not need your permission to start.

The hope is a modest one: little everyday tools that help people connect, create and discover, and leave them feeling a bit more capable and a bit less dependent. If you want to understand one, the code is there. If you want to change it, change it. If you build something better, share it.
