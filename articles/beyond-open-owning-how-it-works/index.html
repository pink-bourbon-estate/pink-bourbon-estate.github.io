Beyond open: owning how it works

I set out to build tools anyone could open and read. Then I noticed that I could not read them either. Open is a start. Understanding is the part that is actually yours.

Why I started

When I opened this site a few days ago I said I wanted to make tools that increase my understanding and decrease my reliance on others: on companies selling my data, on waiting for features and updates, and, more recently, on feeling entirely reliant on AI. If I am honest, I was talking to an AI more than to my partner, and that is not a place I want to be.

So I wanted a site that was completely open. Privacy first, free to view, study and learn from, and human readable: every piece of code something anyone could open and, without falling asleep, follow section by section, with a plain reason for why it does what it does.

The rule for each tool was simple. It should take away one thing I had been leaning on AI for. Recipes and shopping lists. Conversions. Song recommendations. Summarising, compressing, combining. Each one is a small piece of power handed back to me, and to anyone else who wants it.

What has happened since

The more I published, the more I got over my fear of putting myself and this work out there, and the more creative and free I felt. You can see it in the ever-growing list of projects. It is a slow change, and I am only starting to feel its effects, but it is real.

The problem I cannot ignore

Here is the uncomfortable part. I know very little about how any of these tools actually work. I could read the comments in a script, just as an AI can, but I do not think I could write "hello world" from memory right now. Open code that nobody can read is a locked door with a glass panel in it. I can see inside, and I still cannot get in.

I think that is a problem, and it is exactly the dependence I said I wanted to leave.

The radiator

A friend of mine has a classic car. Our world revolves around coffee, so we drive out for one. On the way the radiator starts steaming. We get our coffee and warm ourselves up, he opens the boot and sorts it out, we have a laugh, and we drive home.

Nothing in that story is dramatic, and that is the point. He owns the car in a way that goes beyond having the keys: he knows how it works, so a problem is an afternoon rather than a crisis. That is what I mean by ownership. It is a funny concept, and it leads straight to the right to repair, which we can talk about another day. It is part of why I bought a Pico 2, though maybe not my wisest purchase.

So what about the code? Who gets to be the friend with the boot open?

Tutorials

I have created another repository, called Tutorials. It is empty today. This is what it is for.

Every script here began life as plain Python, for ease, simplicity and speed. Where it mattered I had the AI models move them to Rust or Java. My understanding, and it is a layman's one, is that Rust is strict enough that the code has to be unusually careful before anything runs at all. I would rather ask for that care than hope for it.

The plan is that every project links to its own tutorial. Step by step, in the browser, with an editor, you will build the tool yourself and be told how and why at each step. Every tutorial assumes you know nothing and have never written code. There will be toggles for intermediate and advanced readers, with less explanation. And the path starts before any tool: making a GitHub account, making a website, building this exact website with all its features, submitting it to Google and Bing, and where to put a robots.txt file and what to say in it about AI. Then, one by one, every tool.

The reason is simple. If AI went away tomorrow, the knowledge of how to create, use and change these tools should not go with it.

What I do not know yet

I am also going to explore a helper: you plug in a tool of your own and it writes a tutorial to help you build and edit it. There are no guarantees that this will work well, or at all. It may turn out to need an AI to run, which would be a funny kind of irony, and I will say so plainly if it does.

There is also a lot of writing ahead. Tutorials that teach from zero are hard to get right, and I will get some of them wrong. They will be checked by building them myself, because I am the beginner they are written for. Until they exist, the repository is a promise, not a product.

I thought this fitted the ethos, and I hope it does. Open the code, and then open the door.
